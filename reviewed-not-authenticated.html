The Platner Record — a note on standards

Reviewed Is Not Authenticated

A companion note to the verification ledger. It is about two words that are treated as if they were the same — reviewed and authenticated — and why the difference matters most in the cases where the stakes are highest. This page makes no finding of fact. It examines language, not guilt.

independently verified: false — everywhere in this record, by design.

Two words that are not the same

Much of the public argument about the July 6 allegation — item AL-1 in the ledger — rests on a single sentence from the reporting: that Politico “reviewed email exchanges” between the accuser and her therapist. That sentence is often treated as though it closed the question. It does not — because reviewed and authenticated are different things, and only one of them is a standard of proof.

Reviewed
An editorial judgment

A reporter looked at something and decided it was credible enough to publish. It can be done in an afternoon, on the strength of whatever a source chooses to show.

Authenticated
A standard of proof

An item has been shown, under a defined test, to be what it is claimed to be — genuine, an accurate original, its handling documented. It is answerable to a rule, not a deadline.

This is not an outside standard imported to be unfair to anyone. It is the line this record already draws. The ledger’s own method note says it plainly:

Class labels describe what reporters said they had; they are not a ruling on whether any allegation is true.— verification-ledger.json, method note

What “examined” means here — and what it doesn’t

In this record, the therapist emails are graded Examined. It is worth being exact about what that chip does and does not say. In the ledger’s taxonomy, the meaning is narrow:

Examined — class definition
“Reporter says they personally saw the document/item.”
That is the whole of it. Not authenticated. Not independently confirmed. A reporter’s statement that they looked.

And the ledger entry itself, in its own words, is already careful about how far the item reaches:

AL-1 · Therapist email exchange · independently_verified: false
“Her discussion of the allegation with her therapist — not independent proof of the act. Emails were produced by Racicot.”
The entry also carries a dated correction: an earlier version read the emails as contemporaneous with the 2021 event; Politico’s printed description dates them to June 2026, during her conversations with reporters. The therapist declined to comment.

So the record already holds the line the headlines blur: a reporter says they saw emails the accuser produced, in which she discussed the allegation. That is what examined means. It is not the same as an authenticated record of what happened.

The yardstick: what “authenticated” actually requires

There is no courtroom here — the reporting itself notes there is no police report and no legal proceeding (ledger class Absent). The law is not invoked because anyone is on trial. It is invoked because it is the clearest available yardstick for what it takes to treat a digital record as proof. In Maine, evidence like a text, an email, or a screenshot must pass through two separate doorways before it counts:

Two doorways every digital record must clear
1

Authentication — M.R. Evid. 901. The proponent must produce “evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is.” A low bar, but a real one: a name on an account, or that something “came off the internet,” is not enough on its own (Griffin v. State, Md. 2011; United States v. Vayner, 2d Cir. 2014). Doubts about tampering usually go to weight, not admissibility (State v. Churchill, 2011 ME 121).

2

Best evidence — M.R. Evid. 1001–1004. To prove the content of a message, a printout or screenshot counts as an “original” only “if it accurately reflects the information” (State v. Legassie, 2017 ME 202). In 2025 the Law Court held screenshots need no metadata or expert — but only where their accuracy is undisputed (State v. Pendleton, 2025 ME 40). Where accuracy is contested, that safe harbor falls away.

3

Chain of custody. Gaps go to weight, not admissibility — but that rule assumes no allegation of tampering (State v. Nason, 498 A.2d 252, Me. 1985). The documented trail is what lets everyone trust a record is the record it claims to be.

Set the emails against that yardstick and the point is not that they fail it — no one has tested them either way. The point is that “a reporter reviewed them” does not clear any of these steps. That is not a criticism of the journalism. It is the difference between a newsroom’s judgment and an authenticated record — and it is the difference this whole record was built to keep visible.

“Sort of” is not “confirmed”

The single most-cited piece of corroboration is described, on air, in hedged terms. The words matter, so read them as given:

“We reviewed email exchanges between she and her therapist, referring to what she called this sexual assault and her therapist sort of acknowledging that this had happened to her.”— Adam Wren, Politico, on air (as recorded in AL-1)

“Sort of acknowledging” is not “confirmed.” It is not “the therapist’s notes state.” It is a reporter reaching for an approximation. And the printed version is narrower still. Per Politico’s own description, as recorded in the ledger:

AL-1 · therapist email exchange · Politico, in print
Her therapist responded that Racicot “shouldn’t have to speak publicly about a traumatic incident in order to be believed, without referencing details of any particular incident.”

Even at its strongest, a therapist documents what a patient reports — corroboration that she described this, not that the therapist witnessed it. “Sort of,” and “without referencing details,” are the reporting’s own qualifiers. On a matter this grave, they should be read — not rounded up into certainty.

This cuts in two directions at once, and both belong in the same sentence. There is a person who may be the victim of a sexual assault, and who is owed justice. There is a person who has denied the allegation — “Any accusation of non-consensual behavior is categorically untrue” — and who, if he is wrongly accused, is owed exactly the same. Neither is served by treating “sort of” as if it said “certainly.”

Asking in good faith

We know the objection, because it is usually a fair one: “just asking questions” is one of the oldest ways to smuggle in a conclusion while pretending to stay neutral. So rather than protest our own sincerity, here is the test we hold this page to — and that any reader can apply to it:

  • Does it read the words that were actually said, or invent new ones? Every quotation here is the reporter’s own, or the outlet’s own printed description.
  • Does it apply the same standard to both sides? The same question — authenticated, or only reviewed? — is put to every item, whether it helps the allegation or the denial.
  • Would evidence settle it? Yes. A police finding, a court process, a forensic authentication, a fuller record — any of these would be welcome, and would move this item out of “reviewed” and into something firmer.

A question that passes those three is not an insinuation. It is the minimum diligence a matter this serious deserves — for the accuser and the accused alike.

What this page claims — and what it does not

The standpoint, unchanged

This page makes no finding of fact and takes no position on whether the alleged assault occurred. It does not state or imply that the accuser is lying, or that the allegation is true. It examines the language used to describe the evidence, against the standard by which evidence is normally tested. Consistent with this record’s declared standpoint, Platner’s denial is the operative account until an investigation independent of the publishing outlet corroborates the allegation — which, as of this writing, has not happened.

Reviewed is not authenticated. “Sort of” is not “confirmed.” These are not accusations. They are the difference between someone found this convincing and this has been shown to be what it is claimed to be — a difference that protects the accuser and the accused in equal measure.
